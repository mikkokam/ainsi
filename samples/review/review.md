# The winter timetable is a promise, not a schedule

The islands have no bridge. From November the ferry is the only way a nurse reaches a patient, a child reaches school, and a delivery reaches a shop, which is why the winter timetable is written to be kept in weather that would cancel a summer one.

That promise is what this review measures. Not passengers carried, not fuel burned: departures that happened when the timetable said they would.

> [!NOTE]
> Every figure here is from the bridge log, not the booking system. A crossing that ran empty still counts as kept.

---

# The season in four numbers

<!-- ainsi: figures -->
- **2 848** crossings scheduled between 1 November and 31 March
- **97.4%** departed within ten minutes of the timetable
- **31** cancellations, 22 of them for ice and 9 for mechanical faults
- **0** nights the route was closed end to end

# The nine lost dawns

Every one of the nine was the 05:40 from the mainland, and every one was ice that had drifted into the fairway overnight. The pattern was clear by January and nothing in the timetable changed, because the alternative, moving the first departure to 06:30, breaks the school connection on the far side.

The fix is not in the timetable. It is an icebreaker pass booked before the fairway closes rather than after, which is a phone call at 22:00 and a cost of about €1 800 a night.

# What each failure cost

<!-- ainsi: boxes -->
1. **Bow thruster, twice** €41 000 in parts and a chartered boat for eleven days
2. **March overtime** €28 000 against a budget that assumed a two-week thaw
3. **Nine lost dawns** no cash figure, and the one people remember
